Part: 1
Canto: 26-42

Sumati, the ruler of Visala, warmly welcomes Sage Viswamitra and 
respectfully inquires about Sri Rama and Lakshmana.
He praises their noble appearance, likening their strength and 
grace to that of gods, and admires their youth, valor, and striking presence.
Sumati expresses curiosity about their purpose,
eager to understand why these extraordinary youths,
carrying powerful weapons, have come to his land.
In response, Viswamitra recounts the recent events,
explaining their journey, the challenges faced,
and the defeat of an ogress at Siddhasrama. Sumati,
amazed, honors Sri Rama and Lakshmana with
great respect as guests of high distinction. 
After a restful night in Visala, they depart for Mithila the following day.
On the way, Sri Rama notices an abandoned but beautiful hermitage 
and asks Viswamitra about its history.
Viswamitra, in response, begins to share the tale of the hermitage’s former resident, the revered sage Gautama,
who once lived there with his wife, Ahalya, before the site was cursed.


part: 3
Canto:32-48

The sons of Sage Vasistha, enraged by Trisanku’s persistence after their father’s refusal,
chastise him for seeking another patron despite being rejected by a revered and truthful sage.
They reiterate that Sage Vasistha, as the Ikswaku family’s priest, has the final word in such matters,
and no one should attempt what he has forbidden. They caution that they cannot conduct a sacrifice against his command,
given his authority and wisdom, and advise Trisanku to return to his kingdom.
When Trisanku expresses his intent to approach another priest, they curse him in anger,
reducing him to the form of a Candala—a being of the lowest status. Following the curse, Trisanku’s appearance transforms:
he dons black garments, his fair complexion darkens, and his ornaments and garlands change into those associated with an outcast.
His followers, horrified by his altered form, abandon him.
Alone and in despair, Trisanku seeks out Viswamitra, whose compassionate nature is stirred by the king’s plight.
Seeing Trisanku reduced to this pitiable state, Viswamitra addresses him kindly and inquires about the purpose of his visit.
With humility, Trisanku recounts how he has been rejected by his preceptor and his sons, and now seeks Viswamitra’s help.


Having been refused by the noble Vasistha, TriSanku sought out Vasistha's sons in the hope they would assist him with his sacrifice.
Upon finding them in their austere practices, TriSanku humbly greeted each one with respect, concealing his disappointment
and shame from his previous rejection. Bowing before the sons of his revered preceptor, he asked for their support,
acknowledging them as protectors and placing his trust in them for guidance.
He expressed his deep desire to perform a great sacrifice that would allow him to ascend bodily to heaven.
TriSanku entreated them to bless his endeavor and, bowing with sincerity, implored them to grant him permission.
He conveyed his sense of refuge, explaining that after being denied by Vasistha, they were his last hope to achieve this extraordinary goal.
He appealed to their sense of duty toward the Ikswaku dynasty, reminding them of the pivotal role of their father, Vasistha,
as a spiritual guide to his ancestors, and recognized the sons as the next trusted protectors of his lineage.


After being cursed to become a Candala, Trisanku's transformation was marked by a stark change in appearance,
as his skin and clothing darkened and his adornments changed to those associated with his new status. Deserted by his followers,
Trisanku remained resolute and, alone, journeyed to Viswamitra’s hermitage, placing his hope in the sage’s compassion and strength.
Viswamitra, moved by Trisanku's plight, inquires about the reason for his visit. In response, Trisanku expresses his despair,
stating that despite his adherence to duty and truth, his efforts to ascend to heaven in his bodily form through
a hundred sacrifices have been thwarted. He laments the power of fate, which, in his view, has overshadowed his personal efforts
and prevented his achievements. Trisanku further shares that he has faithfully served his people and honored his elders,
but even this has not helped him avoid his misfortune. Convinced that destiny alone is decisive, Trisanku appeals to Viswamitra,
stating that he has no other refuge and seeking the sage’s help to alter his fate.


Part: 4
Canto: 1-16

Rama, grief-stricken at Sita's absence, 
finds no solace in the beautiful surroundings of Pampa lake.  
He describes the diverse trees and flowers, the cool water filled with lotuses, 
and the various birds and animals that inhabit the area.  
He laments that the very beauty of the place intensifies his pain, 
reminding him of what he has lost.  He wonders how Sita is enduring her captivity, 
and how he will face her father, King Janaka, without her.  
Rama feels unable to bear his sorrow, questioning how he can survive without Sita, 
who had faithfully followed him into exile.  Despite his grief, 
Rama eventually moves on from the lake, searching for any sign of Sita. 
Accompanied by Lakshmana, he explores the forest, its cascades and caves, determined to find her. 
Lakshmana supports and protects Rama, guiding him with advice and demonstrating his strength. 
Meanwhile, Sugriva, king of the monkeys, observes the brothers from Mount Rsyamuka, 
their presence alarming him and his followers, who seek refuge in a nearby hermitage.


Rama continues to lament for Sita, listing more trees and flowering plants near the Pampa lake, 
including Ankola, Kuranta, Curnaka, Paribhadraka, mango, Patali, Kovidara, Mucukunda, Arjuna, Ketaka, Uddalaka, Sirisa, SirmSapa, Dhava, Salmali, red Kurabaka, 
Tinisa, Naktamala, sandalwood, Syandana, Hintala, Tilaka, and Naga. 
 He points out to Lakshmana how the climbers twine around the trees like loving women, 
 and how the wind carries the fragrance of the blossoms.  
 He observes a bee moving from flower to flower, and describes some trees as covered in blooms, 
 others in buds.  He compares the flower-strewn ground to a comfortable bed.  
 He remarks on the beauty of the landscape and the profusion of flowers, 
 comparing the flowering trees to rivals vying for attention.  
 He watches a Karandava bird sporting with its mate, which intensifies his longing for Sita. 
 He states that if Sita were with him, he would desire nothing more, 
 not even the heavens or his kingdom. Lakshmana consoles the grieving Rama, 
 urging him to be strong and reminding him that a pure mind does not falter. 
 He advises Rama to let go of excessive grief, comparing it to a wet wick that 
 burns when doused with too much oil.  Lakshmana assures Rama that Ravana 
 will not escape punishment, even if he hides in the deepest regions. 
 He proposes finding Ravana and either retrieving Sita or defeating him. 
 He vows to kill Ravana even if he takes Sita to the most remote places. 
 Lakshmana emphasizes the power of effort and determination, 
 urging Rama to focus on action rather than despair. 
He reminds Rama of his noble nature and encourages him to rise above his grief.
