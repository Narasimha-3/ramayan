After taking Sunahsepa with him, King Ambarisa rested at Puskara. While the king was resting, Sunahsepa approached his uncle,
the sage Viswamitra, who was engaged in meditation there. Distressed and fatigued,
Sunahsepa sought refuge in Viswamitra's lap and asked for his protection, saying he had no family left to help him.
He requested Viswamitra to find a way for both him to be saved and for the king’s sacrifice to be completed.
Moved by his nephew's plight, Viswamitra consoled Sunahsepa and then appealed to his own sons,
urging them to assist the boy by volunteering in his place for the sacrificial ritual,
thereby upholding the family's honor and duty to assist others. However, Viswamitra's sons responded dismissively,
questioning why he would consider helping someone else’s son over his own. Their words, laced with reluctance and pride,
greatly angered Viswamitra, who saw their refusal as an affront to piety and duty.

After a thousand years of austerities, all the gods, led by Brahma, approach Viswamitra to grant him the fruits of his devotion,
formally recognizing him as a Rsi, or sage, due to his pious acts and perseverance. Satisfied, Brahma returns to the heavens,
leaving Viswamitra to continue his spiritual pursuits.
After some time, Menaka, a beautiful celestial nymph, arrives at Puskara to bathe. Viswamitra, taken by her beauty,
invites her to stay in his hermitage, succumbing to desire. For ten years, they live together in contentment, but eventually,
Viswamitra realizes he has been distracted from his spiritual path, sensing that this was part of the gods' plan to disrupt his focus.
With remorse and frustration, he understands that he has been led astray by passion.
Determined to regain control, Viswamitra dismisses Menaka gently and heads to the Himalayas
to dedicate himself anew to a life of strict celibacy and spiritual discipline. He commits
to intense austerities along the banks of the Kausiki River, drawing the attention and concern of the gods,
who see his renewed devotion as formidable. All the gods, accompanied by the Rsis, approach Brahma,
seeking guidance on how to respond to Viswamitra’s extraordinary dedication.

In response to Sunahsepa's plea, Viswamitra expressed that if his sons would support Sunahsepa,
both the sacrifice could proceed successfully, and Sunahsepa would find a protector. However, Viswamitra's sons, led by Madhucchanda,
opposed this request, questioning why they should offer help to someone outside their family. Their attitude angered Viswamitra,
who then cursed them to be reborn into humble classes, reflecting his disappointment at their lack of piety.
Turning to Sunahsepa, Viswamitra comforted him and taught him two sacred hymns,
instructing him on how to recite them during the ritual to seek the favor of Indra and Vishnu.
When Sunahsepa performed these hymns while bound at the sacrificial post, the gods, pleased with his devotion,
granted him a long life. King Ambarisa, too, was blessed by Indra, achieving the fruits of his sacrifice without harm to Sunahsepa.
Following this, Viswamitra resumed his deep austerities at Puskara, continuing his spiritual practices.
